A complete guide for someone who has never played a board game like this before. Covers the base game, all three expansions, solo play, and the variants people actually use.

Most rulebooks have one setting: everything, at once, in the order the designer filed it. That is a fine reference and a miserable introduction. So this book comes in three depths, and they are nested — each one contains the previous one, expanded.

There is also a glossary at the back. It is not the usual glossary of terms from inside the game — it is a decoder for the jargon that board game people use without noticing, the words that make a rulebook feel like it was written for somebody else.

This book assumes you have never played a modern board game. Every piece of hobby vocabulary gets explained in plain language the first time it appears, in a box like this one. If a term shows up unexplained, that is a bug in the book, not a gap in you.

People describe Wingspan as an engine builder. If you have not met that phrase, it sounds like it belongs to car mechanics, and it is genuinely unhelpful.

Think of a bird feeder in your garden. Hang one feeder and a few birds show up. Those birds attract more birds. Put out a bird bath as well and now you are getting species you never saw before. You did the work once; the yard keeps producing.

In Wingspan, the birds on your board are the feeders. Early on, one bird gets you almost nothing. By the end, taking a single action can set off a chain of five birds in a row, each one handing you something. That chain is your engine. Building an engine means spending your early turns on things that pay you back later, instead of on things that score points right now.

It covers the base game in full, plus the European, Oceania and Asia expansions, the solo opponent, the two-player Duet mode, the six-and-seven-player Flock mode, and a collection of house rules. It is current to those published rules.

It does not reproduce the text of all 470-plus individual bird cards, because that is a card database and not a book. It explains every kind of bird power so that any card you draw will make sense.

The rules in Parts One to Three are checked against the official rulebooks. The strategy chapters are opinion, and they say so. Where experienced players genuinely disagree, this book tells you they disagree rather than pretending there is one answer. The sources are listed at the back.

You are collecting birds. Each bird is a card. You put cards onto a personal board in front of you, and at the end you count up points. Most points wins. There is no combat, nobody attacks you, and almost nothing another player does can hurt you.

On your turn you do exactly one of four things: play a bird from your hand into one of those rows, or use one of the three rows to get food, eggs, or cards. That is the whole menu. One thing, then the next person goes.

To play a bird you pay its cost — some food, shown on the card — and put it in a row. Now the clever bit: a bird sitting in a row makes that row better. An empty forest gets you one food. A forest with four birds in it gets you three food, plus each of those birds may do its own little trick. So the more birds you have in a row, the more a single turn produces.

The game lasts four rounds, and the rounds get shorter: eight turns, then seven, then six, then five. Twenty-six turns total. That shrinking clock is the real opponent. Every bird you play costs a turn you could have spent scoring instead.

Points come from: the number printed on each bird you played, one point per egg sitting on your birds, one point for various bits and pieces your birds collect, four public goals that reward things like "most birds in the wetland", and one secret goal card of your own.

Eggs are worth knowing about early, because they do two jobs. They are one point each at the end — and they are also money. Putting a bird into the better slots on your board costs eggs. So every egg is a small argument with yourself.

Food comes out of a dice tower shaped like a bird feeder. You take a die, you get the food shown on it. You cannot get what has not been rolled, which is the main source of swearing.

Roughly: spend the early game getting set up, spend the late game cashing in. By the last round you are mostly laying eggs, because there is no time left for a new bird to pay you back.

Two. Look at the four public goal tiles before you do anything. They are face up from the start and they tell you what the game will reward.

It takes about an hour. It is quiet, it is pretty, and the birds are real — every card is an actual species with a genuine fact on it. You will lose the first one. That is fine; that is not really what the first game is for.

Part One gave you the shape. This part gives you the game. It covers the base box only — no expansions — because that is what you should play first, and because everything the expansions add makes more sense once this is solid.

Wingspan is played over four rounds. In each round, everyone takes turns going clockwise until they run out of turns, then the round ends and you score a goal. Then the next round.

The reason for the shrinking is worth knowing, because it makes the number stick. You have eight little wooden cubes. Each turn you take, you spend one — but you get them all back at the end of the round, except one: at the end of each round you permanently park one cube on the scoring board to mark how you did on that round's goal. It never comes back. Eight cubes, then seven, then six, then five.

If you hear someone say tableau, they mean the spread of cards in front of you — your personal collection on the table, as opposed to the cards hidden in your hand. In Wingspan your tableau is the birds you have played onto your board. Cards in your hand are worth nothing; cards in your tableau are worth points and do things.

The goal board is double-sided. The green side is a competition: first, second and third place on each goal. The blue side just gives one point per thing you have, up to five, with no comparing.

Green is the default and it is the better game. Blue is gentler and much less swingy. For a table of complete beginners, use blue. Nobody gets flattened, and you can switch to green next time.

Take a player board (the big one with three rows of birds on it), eight action cubes in your colour, five bird cards, two bonus cards, and five food tokens — one of each of the five food types.

Keep four birds and you keep one food. Keep two birds and you keep three food. Keep all five birds and you keep no food at all, which is a disaster.

Then look at your two bonus cards — secret personal goals, scored once at the very end — and keep one, discarding the other. You may look at your bonus cards while choosing your birds; that is the point.

Keep two or three birds, and the food that matches them. This is the single most common beginner mistake and it is worth being blunt about: birds you cannot feed are cardboard. They sit in your hand doing nothing while you spend turns hunting for food you needed three turns ago.

Each row has five slots for bird cards. You fill them strictly left to right — you cannot skip an empty slot to reach a nicer one, and a bird once played can never be moved or removed.

When you use that row, you put your cube on the leftmost space that is still uncovered. So the more birds in the row, the further right you start — and further right pays better.

Notice the rhythm. Your first, third and fifth bird in a row each unlock a trade. Your second and fourth each raise the raw amount. Nothing is wasted, but the birds do different jobs depending on where they land.

With five birds, every action space in the row is covered — so each row has one more, narrow action space printed past the end of the fifth slot. That is what you use. It gives the row's best output plus its trade. A full row is the strongest version of that row, not a broken one.

This is on top of the bird's food cost, and the eggs come off birds already on your own board — not from the shared supply. You cannot part-pay. Since each egg is also worth a point at the end, a column-four bird quietly costs you two points before it has done anything.

Three of the four actions are the same action wearing different hats. The fourth is the odd one out, and confusing them is the most common rules error there is.

Playing a bird is the only action that does not set off a row. You do not activate the other birds in that habitat. Nothing happens except the new card's own "when played" text, if it has any.

When paying a bird's food cost — and only then — you may spend any two food tokens as if they were any one food token. The two do not have to match each other. A seed and a fish together can buy one rodent.

This is your escape hatch when the dice refuse to give you what you need. It does not work anywhere else in the game — in particular it does not work for bird abilities that ask you to spend food.

A bird further right can produce exactly the thing a bird further left wants to spend. Resolve them left-to-right — the way you read — and the chain silently breaks. Physically slide the cube; that is what it is for.

Every ability is optional, and so is every benefit. The official phrasing is worth memorising: you can always decline a benefit, but you can never decline a cost. You may lay fewer eggs than shown, take less food, skip an ability entirely.

You get them from the birdfeeder, a dice tower holding five dice. Take one die out, gain one matching token. The die stays out until the feeder is refilled.

Each die has six faces: the five foods, plus one split face showing invertebrate or seed. That split face gives you one token of whichever you prefer — not one of each. Count the faces and the economy of the whole game falls out:

That is why a bird costing two worms is dramatically easier to play than one costing two fish, and it is a real reason to favour worm-and-seed birds in your opening hand.

You take dice one at a time. So if taking your first die leaves the tray all showing one face, you may reset the feeder before choosing your second. Officially clarified, and easy to miss.

An egg on one of your birds is worth one point at the end. It is also the currency for columns two through five. You cannot have it both ways, and noticing that tension is most of playing well.

When you lay eggs you spread them across your birds however you like — all on one bird, or spread out. But every bird has an egg limit printed on it (little egg icons down the left edge). A bird at its limit takes no more, and any eggs you cannot place are simply lost.

Your only birds are two with a limit of two eggs each, and they already hold three eggs between them. You take the grassland action for three eggs.

Each bird also has a nest type: platform, bowl, cavity or ground. Nests do nothing during play — they exist only for goals and bonus cards. Some birds have a star nest, which is wild and counts as all four types. Those are quietly excellent.

You draw from the three face-up cards in the tray or blind from the top of the deck, in any mix. The face-up cards are not replaced until the end of your turn, so if you draw two you can take two of the three visible ones.

A bird card only ever scores by being played onto your board, or by being tucked — slid face down underneath a bird by an ability that does that — where it is worth one point. Exactly one bonus card in the whole deck rewards a full hand. Everything else punishes it.

At the end of each round all three face-up cards are discarded and replaced. If you have been eyeing one and not taking it, that is your deadline.

Every card has the same anatomy: habitat and food cost top-left, points top-right, nest type and egg limit down the left edge, a wingspan measurement, a real fact about the bird, and usually an ability. The colour of the ability tells you when it works — and that tells you what the bird is worth.

Brown is the engine. A brown ability is not worth what it does once; it is worth what it does multiplied by how many more times you will take that action. Played in round one it might fire eight or ten times. Played in round four it fires once.

Pink is free money. It happens on other people's turns, so it costs you no action at all. In a four-player game there are a lot of those. The cap is firm though — once between your own turns, no matter how many opponents set it off. Tell the table what your pink birds do, so someone remembers.

Two other things birds do, both worth one point each at the end: caching food (putting a food token on the bird, where it can never be spent) and tucking cards underneath themselves.

Points come from six places. Four of them are sitting face up on the table from the moment you start, which makes Wingspan far less improvised than it first looks.

Four goal tiles are laid out during setup — one per round, all face up from the beginning. You know from turn one exactly what will be rewarded and when. Not reading them is the biggest free mistake available.

They ask for things like: most birds in the wetland, most eggs in the forest, most birds with bowl nests that have at least one egg on them, most birds played in total, or most complete sets of eggs (one in each habitat).

On a tie, tied players share a place: both cubes go on it, the next place down is not awarded, and at the end you add that place's value to the next place's value, divide by how many tied, and round down. Two players tying for first on a 5 / 2 / 1 goal get three each, and nobody takes second.

Your bonus card is the secret one you chose during setup. They reward things like birds with a wingspan over 65cm, birds worth fewer than four points, birds that only live in the wetland, birds with colours in their names, or birds with at least one egg on them. Have a look at yours early — it may nudge which birds you keep.

This is as close to consensus as Wingspan strategy gets. A row with four birds gives you a bigger basic action and chains four abilities every single time you use it. Two rows with two birds each gives you neither. Beginners spread out because it feels balanced and safe; it is neither.

Fill one habitat toward four or five before you seriously branch out. Which habitat? Experienced players genuinely disagree — one camp says forest first, because you cannot play anything at all without food; another says grassland first, because eggs both buy the expensive columns and score most of the goals. Both work. Spreading across all three does not.

Rounds one and two: buy capacity. Birds with brown abilities, food, cards. You will feel like you are not scoring. You are not. That is correct.

The same card is a great buy in round one and a poor one in round four. A two-point bird with a strong brown ability that fires ten times beats a seven-point bird that does nothing — early. With two turns left, that reverses completely, because the ability has nowhere left to run.

The bird is worth 6, but column four costs two eggs — and eggs are a point each, so you are destroying two points to gain six. Net: +4.

Genuinely close. Now check the goal tile: if round four rewards eggs, the eggs win outright. This is the kind of small sum that decides real games, and "never play birds late" is too blunt to be useful.

You will probably keep too many birds at setup, spread across all three habitats, and discover in round three that nothing is producing much. Everyone does this. It is almost a rite of passage, and it is why the second game feels like a completely different, much better game.

All abilities are optional. The governing principle: you can always decline a benefit; you can never decline a cost. You may lay fewer eggs, take less food, or draw fewer cards than a space offers.

Caching puts a food token onto a bird, where it is worth one point at the end and can never be spent. If food tokens run out, cache discarded cards as substitutes. Tucking slides a card face down under a bird, worth one point each. Some cards show a predator icon or a flocking icon; these matter only because other cards and bonus cards refer to them.

Where a card says to "repeat" another bird's ability in the same habitat, you activate that other bird's power — you do not copy it onto yourself.

When all cubes are placed, in order: score the round's goal tile, marking your result with one action cube on the goal board (even if you score zero — it goes on the 0 space); remove all action cubes from your board; discard and replace all three face-up bird cards; pass the first-player token clockwise.

Final scoring: printed bird values, bonus card, end-of-round goals, and one point each for eggs on birds, food cached on birds, and cards tucked under birds. Most points wins; ties go to the most unused food tokens; still tied, the victory is shared.

Ties: tied players occupy the same place, and the next place down is not awarded. At the end, add the tied place's value to the next place's value (or places, for a three-way tie), divide by the number tied, and round down.

Bonus cards are secret, chosen one from two at setup, and scored once at the very end. Several bird cards let you draw more of them mid-game. Each card shows what percentage of the bird deck qualifies — genuinely useful information when deciding which of your two to keep.

Published guides disagree — one puts a typical bonus card around seven points, another puts a well-executed one at eight to thirteen. A useful mental model for a first few games. Your bird values and your eggs will usually be the two biggest columns on the scorepad. Goals are the swingiest — three contested goals can easily be a twelve-point spread between first and third. Cached food and tucked cards are the quiet accumulators: individually tiny, but a bird that tucks a card every time you use a row will out-earn its printed value several times over.

This is why "which bird is best" is an unanswerable question. A bird is worth its printed points, plus its eggs, plus whatever its ability accumulates, plus how much it advances the row it sits in, plus whether it feeds a goal or your bonus card. The printed number is the smallest part of that.

You may take the same action many turns in a row. New players sometimes assume they must vary their action, or must use each row once. There is no such rule, and repeating the same habitat action is often exactly right.

Wingspan gives you twenty-six turns. Every turn you spend must either produce points or increase what later turns produce. A turn that does neither is the only real way to lose.

All four goals are public from setup. They are not four separate puzzles — they are a schedule telling you what the game rewards and when.

Because the payout climbs from 4 to 7 for first place, a goal in round four is worth nearly twice one in round one. If you can only contest some of them, contest the late ones. And check which goals you currently score zero on — going from zero to one of something is often worth more than going from three to four, because zero scores nothing at all.

Pink "once between turns" abilities fire on other players' turns. They cost you no action. In a four-player game there are three opponent turns between each of yours; over a full game that is a substantial pile of resources you never paid a turn for.

They scale with player count, which is worth knowing: pink birds are excellent at five players and nearly worthless in a two-player game or against the solo opponent.

Tempo means how much you are getting done per turn compared to everyone else. You "lose tempo" when you spend a turn on something that does not move you forward — drawing cards you never play, laying eggs with nowhere to put them, playing a bird whose ability never fires.

An Automa is a deck of cards that plays the part of an opponent. You flip a card, do what it says on the opponent's behalf, and carry on. It has no strategy and makes no decisions — it just applies pressure, so that your solo game still has a score to beat and a race to lose.

The name comes from the Italian for "automaton". It was invented for a different Stonemaier game, Viticulture, and has appeared in most of their games since.

That last point cuts both ways, and it is the rule people forget: if one of your abilities compares you to other players, the Automa loses that comparison. If an ability gives other players something, the Automa gains nothing.

The Automa scores: end-of-round goals, printed values of its face-up birds, 3/4/5 points per face-down bird by difficulty, and one point per egg. It does not score its bonus card. On a tie it counts as holding two leftover food tokens.

The Automa collects birds and eggs faster than you can, and it looks alarming. It is meant to. Its eggs are competing against your eggs plus your tucked cards and cached food, and its birds have to account for the bonus card points you will score and it will not.

Pink abilities become nearly worthless. They only fire when an Automa card explicitly says to activate them, which is a small fraction of turns. In multiplayer they are free income; solo they are close to dead weight.

The bird tray is violent. The Automa's Draw Cards action wipes all three face-up cards. If there is a card you want, take it — waiting a turn genuinely loses it.

The feeder gets stripped. Its Gain Food action removes every die of one face. Planning two turns ahead around a specific food is much riskier than in a multiplayer game.

Each expansion adjusts the solo rules. The European Expansion adds two Automa-only bonus cards you may choose instead of drawing randomly, plus scoring cards for its new goals; its "steal" abilities can only take from the Automa if it has cubes on the current goal tile, and for the new round-end abilities that ask you to choose a player, the Automa's quantity equals its cubes on the current goal tile. The Oceania and Asia expansions each ship their own Automa rulebooks, with Asia's addressing the Duet goals that replace the standard ones.

When you spend nectar, it does not go back to the supply — it goes onto the "spent nectar" space of the row where you spent it. If you spent it playing a bird, the row you played the bird in; otherwise the row you activated that turn.

At the end of the game, in each habitat separately, the player with the most spent nectar scores 5 points and second-most scores 2. You need at least one token in a habitat to qualify. Ties add the values and divide, rounded down — two players tied for most get 3 each.

Asia is three products in one box: a standalone game for one or two players, a card expansion for any other Wingspan game, and a 6–7 player expansion when combined with the base box.

Its cards carry an "A" in the bottom-right corner and mix freely with everything else. If you already know Wingspan there are no new rules to learn unless you play Duet or Flock mode. Its rulebook marks Duet instructions in red boxes and Flock instructions in blue.

Asia also includes a Duet Swift-Start system: six cards with red corners and a guide per player that walks both players through their opening turns. It is the single best way to teach the game to one other person.

All the decks are designed to be shuffled together, and each is internally balanced to keep habitats and nest types evenly spread and to keep the bonus card percentages roughly honest. You can also mix any two decks and leave the third out. The corner icons let you separate them again later.

Practically: with everything mixed you will have well over 400 bird cards, a six-food economy, five ability colours, and an end-of-round sequence with six steps. It is a lot. It is also, for people who like Wingspan, the best version of it.

It is called Wyrmspan, and the most important thing to know is what it is not: it is not a Wingspan expansion, it does not shuffle into your bird deck, and none of its components mix with Wingspan's. Designed by Connie Vogelmann, developed by Elizabeth Hargrave (who designed Wingspan), illustrated by Clémentine Campardou, published by Stonemaier Games. One to five players, around 90 minutes, ages 14+.

Wyrmspan is meaningfully more complex than Wingspan — more actions, more subsystems, more decisions per turn. If you are reading this book because you are new to board games, play Wingspan for a good while first. Wyrmspan is a fine second game and a punishing first one.

If you already like Wingspan and want the same feeling with more to chew on, it is the obvious next purchase. There is an expansion for it, Dragon Academy, which adds dragon guilds.

None of them, strictly. Wyrmspan re-derives its rules rather than reskinning Wingspan's, so there is no lookup table between the two. The habit worth carrying across is only the deep one: fill a row, because a full row does more per action. The specific numbers, costs and timings are all its own.

An expansion needs the original box to work — it adds cards or systems to a game you already own. A standalone is a complete game you can buy and play by itself.

Some boxes are both. Wingspan Asia is a standalone game and an expansion, depending on how you use it. Wyrmspan is only a standalone; it has no expansion relationship to Wingspan whatsoever, despite the name and the family resemblance.

None of these are official. All of them are played by somebody. Each one is listed with the problem it is trying to solve, because a house rule adopted without a problem usually creates one.

Wingspan is unusually well balanced, and most of the "problems" people fix with house rules are actually first-game experiences. Play three or four games as written before you start adjusting. The two official knobs — the blue goal board and the Automa difficulty levels — solve more than people expect.

Just one: play your first game with hands face up and the blue goal board. Both are permitted by the official rules, neither changes the game's structure, and together they remove nearly all of the misery of a first game. Everything else on this list can wait until you have a reason.

Every card is an actual species with an actual fact on it, sourced from actual ornithologists. This is where the game stops being about cubes.

Most games with a theme have a theme in the way a hotel has a carpet. Wingspan does not. The bird information comes from the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, the Audubon field guides and the Sibley guides; the European cards draw on the Handbook of the Birds of the World and the Collins Bird Guide. Somebody checked.

The American Kestrel is following mouse wee across a field, using ultraviolet vision, and then filing the results. This is a bird the size of a coffee mug.

The Griffon Vulture, wingspan 252cm, has a bald head for temperature control. The other reason is not printed on the card, and you can work it out.

On food types being a simplification: the rulebook apologises that the five food types are "very generalized", noting that the invertebrate symbol is a caterpillar, but some invertebrate-eaters actually specialise in flying insects, or swimming insects, or shellfish. This is a note about a cardboard token shaped like a worm.

On egg limits: every bird's egg limit is proportionate to how many eggs that species really lays — scaled down, because birds with a limit of six in the game "may actually lay 15 or more eggs in a year". The scoring system is a scale model of avian reproduction.

On nectar, in Oceania, the designer explains that nectar is a wild food because sugar attracts insects and mammals, and pollinated flowers become fruit and seed — so nectar's presence really does lead to other food. The note ends: "When you feed nectar to a hawk, imagine it finding a sugar glider in a flowering eucalyptus!" That is a rules justification with an ecosystem behind it.

On the robot's name: the solo opponent is called the Automa, from the Italian for "automaton", because the first one Stonemaier built was for a game set in Italy. It has been called that in every game since, in every country.

Because the birds are real, the categories are real: nest types, wingspans, diets and habitats are properties the birds genuinely have, not properties invented to make a puzzle. That is why the bonus cards can ask for something as arbitrary-sounding as "birds with body parts in their names" and still feel fair — the names were already there.

It is also why Wingspan is unusually pleasant to lose. You spend an hour handling small pictures of real animals with true facts on them, and at the end someone counts higher than you. The counting is genuinely the least interesting thing that happened.

Read the fact out loud when you play a bird. It takes four seconds, it is the best part of the game, and a table that does this has a completely different evening from a table that does not.

Wingspan has a lot of arbitrary numbers — five action tracks, an egg-cost ladder, a scoring table that climbs. None of it is hard, all of it is forgettable. What follows is crude on purpose, because crude sticks. Skip the chapter if it is not your sort of thing; nothing here is load-bearing.

Forest gives food — same letter, free of charge. Grassland is where you lay eggs. Wetland is where you draw cards, and "withdraw" is close enough to stick. If you only take one thing from this chapter, take that.

Both the Great Tit and the Coal Tit are real cards in the European Expansion, so you can say this at the table and claim you were discussing ornithology.

Your 1st, 3rd and 5th bird in a row each unlock an optional trade — a tease, something you may do. Your 2nd and 4th each raise the actual quantity. So if you want more stuff rather than more options, you want an even number of birds in that row.

Right to left. Your cube goes on the rightmost available space and works backwards, so the last bird you played fires first. Getting this backwards silently breaks combinations, and this is the only mnemonic in the chapter that will actually save you points.

Any two tokens count as any one token — but only when paying a bird's printed food cost. Abilities want exactly what they ask for.

Three rules in one sentence: wild for any cost, not wild when an ability names a specific food, and discarded from your supply at the end of every round.

Odd, rude or vivid material is recalled better than neutral material — psychologists call it the bizarreness effect, and it is the same reason every medical student in the world knows a truly disgusting mnemonic for the cranial nerves. You will forget "the grassland track is 2, 2, 3, 3, 4" by Tuesday. You will not forget which row is bigger.

The exact payout of each action space — 1/1/2/2/3 food, 2/2/3/3/4 eggs, 1/1/2/2/3 cards, plus the sixth space past the fifth slot — is printed on the player board and reproduced in the rulebook's board illustration, but the rulebook's body text never tabulates it. Those numbers were read from the official board artwork. If your board disagrees with this book, believe your board.

The strategy chapters, the "which expansion first" advice and the commentary on house rules are judgement, not rules. They draw on published guides from Meeple Mountain, Cats and Dice, King Panda Games and Wingsplain, plus BoardGameGeek's house-rule collections.

Where those sources genuinely disagree — which habitat to lead with, and how hard to build around your bonus card — this book presents the disagreement rather than pretending one side won. Where they agree, it says so.
