A complete guide for a working engineer who has never built with LLMs.Covers the mental model, the APIs, tools and agents, retrieval, evals, and what production does to all of it.

Most technical material about AI arrives at one of two broken settings: breathless (everything is about to change, no APIs shown) or exhaustive (four hundred pages, half of them stale by the time you read them). This book tries a third setting, and it comes in nested depths — each one complete in itself, each one containing the previous one, expanded.

This book assumes you are a competent engineer and a complete newcomer to LLMs. Every piece of AI-industry vocabulary gets decoded in plain language the first time it appears, in a box like this one, and again in the glossary at the back. If a term shows up unexplained, that is a bug in the book, not a gap in you.

People describe model limits, prices, and speeds in tokens, and if you let the word slide past, half the engineering in this book stays fuzzy.

A token is a chunk of text about three or four characters long — a short word, or a piece of a longer one. Models do not read letters or words; text is chopped into these chunks before the model ever sees it, and generated back out one chunk at a time.

Think of tokens as the metered unit of everything: the context window is measured in them, the invoice is denominated in them, latency scales with how many of them the model must produce. When this book says “that costs tokens,” it means literally: someone is paying, in money and milliseconds, per chunk.

It covers what a mid-career engineer needs to build competently with large language models in 2026: the working mental model, the converged API surface across the three major vendors, prompting that survives production, tool use and the agent loop, retrieval and when to skip it, evaluation, and operations.

It does not cover training or fine-tuning your own models beyond knowing when to reach for them, the research frontier, or anything that changes weekly — model names, prices, token limits. Where those matter, the book names the stable concept and points at the vendors' living documentation. The sources chapter says what was verified against what, and its annotated bibliography is the reading list for going deeper.

The engineering judgments in Part Three are opinions, clearly labelled, and where practitioners genuinely disagree — retrieval pipelines versus agentic search, build versus buy for observability — the book states both cases at full strength instead of pretending there is one answer.

You are going to call a very capable text-prediction function over HTTPS. That is the entire mechanical situation. A large language model takes a sequence of tokens — text, chopped into chunks — and predicts what comes next, over and over, until an answer exists. Everything else in this field is that loop wearing better and better clothes.

Three properties of that function will shape all of your engineering. It is sampled: the same input can produce different outputs on different runs, so your systems must validate, retry, and measure rather than assume. It is stateless: the model remembers nothing between calls; whatever it should know must be sent inside the request, every time, and sending text costs money per token. It is trained, not programmed: instructions shift its behaviour powerfully but probabilistically — a rule in prose is a strong suggestion, never a guarantee.

From those three facts, the whole discipline unfolds. You will write prompts — which are configuration files in English, and belong in version control. You will demand structured output — the model filling in your JSON Schema rather than writing essays your code must parse. You will give the model tools: functions it can ask your code to run. Read that carefully — ask. The model never executes anything; it emits a request, your code decides, and everything trustworthy about AI systems lives in that gap.

Chain tool calls in a loop and you have an agent: a model working toward a goal, choosing its next step from what the last step revealed. Agents are genuinely useful and genuinely variable, and the craft is bounding them — budgets, permissions, sandboxes, a human at the irreversible moments.

When the model must answer from your documents rather than its training data, you will put those documents in the request — all of them if they fit, or the relevant slices found by search if they do not. The industry calls the second pattern RAG, and its failures are almost always search failures, not intelligence failures.

And because the function is sampled, you will need evals: a versioned set of real test cases with a grading function, run on every change, because a one-word prompt edit can move quality ten points in either direction and nothing but measurement will tell you which. Teams that skip this are doing vibes-driven development against a random number generator, and it goes how you would expect.

In production, the things that actually break first: cost (tokens multiply quietly), silent model changes (the vendor improves things out from under you), and the long tail of weird inputs. All three are handled with ordinary engineering — telemetry, budgets, pinned versions, fallbacks — applied to an unusual dependency.

That is the whole shape. The model is brilliant and unreliable; your job is systems that get the brilliance out while containing the unreliability. It is a genuinely good engineering problem, and none of it requires believing anyone's press release.

Everything starts as one HTTPS POST. You send a transcript: a system turn (your standing instructions — persona, rules, output expectations), then user and assistant turns alternating. The response is a new assistant message, plus two fields your code must actually read: stop_reason, which says why generation ended, and usage, which counts the tokens you just bought.

The API is stateless. A “conversation” means you re-send the whole transcript, grown by two turns, every round. Two consequences arrive immediately: token costs grow with conversation length, and prompt caching — the provider re-reading your unchanged request prefix at a steep discount — is not an optimisation so much as the pricing model. Keep the stable parts of your request (system prompt, tool definitions, big documents) first and byte-identical across calls; put the moving parts last. One timestamp at the top of your system prompt disables caching for the entire request.

The three vendors — Anthropic (Messages API), OpenAI (Responses API), Google (Interactions API) — converged on this same shape: typed transcript in, message out, tools described in JSON Schema, streaming over server-sent events, schema-enforced output, caching, and a half-price asynchronous batch tier. Learn the shape once; wrap the vendor differences behind one thin module on day one, because section eight of the full reference will want a fallback chain and future-you will want the option to switch.

Decide what the output is before you write a word of instruction. If a program consumes it, the answer is a JSON Schema, enforced by the provider's structured output mode — the model literally cannot emit tokens that violate the schema — and validated again in your code on receipt, because defence in depth is free here.

Then the prompt, which is a configuration file in English and gets treated like one: version-controlled, reviewed, tested. The layout that works: durable rules in the system turn, numbered and few; the material fenced in explicit tags (<document>…</document>) so instructions and data cannot blur; the task stated after the material — models attend most reliably to the edges of long contexts — and an escape hatch, always: “if the document does not contain the answer, return null.” A model cornered into answering will answer; whether the answer exists is your problem to design for, and the escape hatch is the single cheapest hallucination reduction available.

Add two or three input→output examples when format or labelling conventions matter — show the weird cases, not the easy ones — and skip examples for open-ended reasoning, where they mostly teach the model to parrot. When a task genuinely requires multi-step reasoning (debugging, planning, auditing), the vendors sell a thinking dial that buys accuracy at the price of latency and tokens; leave it off for extraction and formatting, and let measurements — not vibes — decide where it stays on.

Tools turn the text predictor into something that acts. You describe functions — name, description, parameter schema — and the model, mid-conversation, stops and emits a structured request: call search_orders with these arguments. Your code executes the real function and returns the result as a new turn; the model reads it and continues, perhaps with another tool call, until it answers. That cycle is the runtime of every AI agent you will ever meet.

Hold the load-bearing fact: the model never executes anything. It asks. Your executor — the code that receives those structured requests — is the security boundary and the reliability boundary. It validates arguments like they came from the internet (they did, laundered through a model), enforces permissions and budgets, executes with its own scoped credentials, and returns errors as instructive content (“date range too wide — split the query”) rather than crashing the loop, because models recover from well-written errors startlingly well.

Write tool descriptions like API docs for a very fast, very literal contractor: what it does, when to use it, what the results mean, what not to do (“empty list means no such order — do not retry with guessed ids”). That last sentence class prevents more bad agent behaviour than any system-prompt rule.

And the rule that keeps you off the incident channel: anything that must never happen gets made impossible in code, not forbidden in prose. A prompt saying “never delete data” is a prior; an executor with no delete verb is a fact. When the stakes are real — sending, merging, paying — the pattern is a permission tier: reads auto-approve, reversible writes auto-approve with logging, irreversible actions wait for a human or a deterministic policy check.

When the model must answer from your material, put the material in the context. If the corpus fits comfortably and changes slowly — a handbook, one product's docs — send all of it and let caching make that cheap. No search step means nothing to miss.

When the corpus outgrows the window, retrieve: search for the relevant slices at question time, insert them with source ids, and instruct the model to answer only from what was provided, citing which, and to say so when the answer is not there. The searching is ordinary information retrieval — keyword search for identifiers, embedding search for paraphrase, both fused for real systems — and when a retrieval-backed bot answers wrongly, the retrieval usually missed before the model ever spoke. Debug the search first, always.

Before you ship, collect twenty to fifty real cases — inputs plus expected outcomes stated as behaviour (“declines and cites the policy”), not exact wording — and run them on every change to prompt, schema, or model. Grade with code wherever possible (schema validity, required fields, the cited source exists); use a model grading against a written rubric for qualities code cannot check, after you have verified the grader agrees with humans on a labelled sample.

Then the ratchet, which is the closest thing this field has to a silver bullet: every production failure becomes a test case before its fix merges. The same failure, once seen, can never return silently. Fifty cases grown this way outperform five hundred written in a vacuum, and they are what makes next quarter's model upgrade a diff you read instead of a fire you fight.

Part Three begins where every incident review ends: with the mental model. This chapter is the five facts about the machine that every later chapter leans on — read it even if you skim everything else.

You do not need to know how a transformer works. You need five load-bearing facts about what is on the other end of the HTTP call, because every production incident traces back to forgetting one of them.

An LLM is a function that takes a sequence of tokens and returns a probability distribution over the next token. The provider samples from that distribution, appends the result, and runs the function again, until a stop condition. That is the whole machine. Everything you will ship — chat, extraction, tool-calling agents — is that loop wearing increasingly elaborate clothing.

The instinct this should trigger is not “so it’s just autocomplete” — sequence prediction at this scale turns out to do real reasoning, real code review, real planning, and being smug about the substrate will not help you debug anything. The instinct it should trigger is: the output is sampled, not computed. Same input, two different runs, two different answers. You are integrating a stochastic dependency, and everything downstream — retries, evals, monitoring — follows from taking that seriously.

The last row generalises into the most useful planning distinction in this whole discipline: weights versus context. What is in the weights is a compressed, dated, lossy memory of training data — fluent, broad, and unreliable at the edges. What is in the context window the model can actually attend to, quote, and be graded against. When accuracy matters, your job is almost always to move the load-bearing facts out of the weights and into the context.

Hallucination is not a bug to file; it is the default failure mode of sampling. A model completing text will complete it plausibly whether or not the facts exist — a confident citation to a paper that was never written looks exactly like a confident citation to one that was. You do not fix this with a sterner system prompt. You fix it with grounding (give it the source), verification (check the output), and product design that keeps a human in the loop where it counts. Section seven turns this into tests; section eight turns it into monitoring.

One more calibration, because it sets expectations for everything that follows: capability and reliability are different axes. A 2026 frontier model can write a working parser on the first try, and can also fail the same request one time in fifty in a way no compiler ever would. Engineering for the second fact — not marvelling at the first — is most of what this lesson is about. The jagged edge between what works ten-out-of-ten and what works nine-out-of-ten is invisible in a demo and decisive in production.

With the mental model in place, the wire format. This chapter is the longest-lived knowledge in the book: vendors rename products yearly, but the converged shape — transcript in, message out, schema-enforced output, cached prefixes — has held stable and is held in common.

Three vendors, one converged shape: messages in, a message out, tools described in JSON Schema, streaming over SSE, caching for the parts you repeat. Learn the shape once and the brand differences become footnotes.

Those last two fields are not telemetry garnish. stop_reason tells you why generation ended — finished naturally (end_turn), hit your token ceiling (max_tokens, meaning the output is truncated and probably unparseable), or wants to call a tool (tool_use, section four). usage is your bill and your latency, itemised. Production code branches on the first and logs the second, always.

Every vendor’s request is a typed transcript. The system turn is yours: durable posture, rules, persona — highest-privilege instructions the model is trained to weight above the conversation. The user turns carry the task and the data. The assistant turns are the model’s own prior outputs, replayed back so it can see what it already said — and because the API is stateless, you replay them, every call. One practical consequence engineers meet in week one: put the ten-page document in a user turn (it is material), keep the system turn for standing orders (it is policy). A second consequence: nothing stops you writing words into the assistant turns yourself — prefilling the start of a reply to force a format, or editing history the model believes it produced. The transcript is an input you fabricate, not a log you are stuck with.

Names differ; the row headings are the durable part. Two of them deserve immediate attention because they change how you design, not just what you spend.

Structured outputs. All three vendors can now constrain generation to a JSON Schema you supply — not “please respond in JSON,” but decode-time enforcement where non-conforming tokens are never sampled. This converts the fuzziest part of LLM integration (parsing prose) into a typed contract. The 2026 default for any machine-consumed output is: define the schema, enforce it at the API, validate it anyway on receipt (defence in depth — and providers still bail to prose refusals in edge cases), and treat schema design as prompt design, because field names and descriptions steer the model as much as any instruction.

Prompt caching. Providers will cache the unchanged prefix of your request — system prompt, tool definitions, long documents, conversation-so-far — and charge a fraction for reading it back (cache writes cost a premium; the economics only work when reuse is real). The catch that bites everyone once: caching is prefix-based. The request is cached left-to-right up to the first changed byte, so order your requests stable-first — system prompt and tools, then documents, then the moving parts. Put a timestamp in the first line of your system prompt and you have disabled caching for the whole request. In multi-turn agents (section six), where the same growing transcript is re-sent every step, cache design is frequently the difference between a viable unit cost and an absurd one.

Streaming is a UX decision, then a parsing chore. With stream: true you get server-sent events: text deltas, tool-call argument fragments, a final usage tally. Time-to-first-token is what users feel, so anything conversational streams. The chore: your handler must accumulate partial JSON for tool arguments and cope with events it does not recognise — vendors add event types without asking. For non-interactive pipelines, skip the chore: request the complete response, or use the batch tier and collect results asynchronously at half price.

Last durable fact: capability lives per-model, not per-vendor. Every provider ships a frontier tier, a fast mid tier, and a cheap small tier, with different context ceilings, latencies, and per-token prices. Now the part everyone thinks is the whole field, cut down to the part that is actually engineering. Prompts as versioned configuration, contracts before instructions, and the validate-retry loop that outperforms any amount of wording folklore.

The internet is full of prompt “tricks.” Ignore them. What survives contact with production is a small set of disciplines: specify the output shape, place instructions where they hold, show examples only when they earn their tokens, and build the retry loop before you need it.

Recalibrate the word first. A production prompt is not a chat message; it is configuration that executes — often the highest-leverage file in the service. It belongs in version control, it goes through review, it gets a changelog entry when it changes, and — section seven — it has tests, because a one-word edit can move a success rate ten points in either direction and nothing but an eval will tell you which.

Most bad prompts fail before the first instruction: they never say what the output is. Start every serious prompt from the contract — the schema if output is machine-consumed (section two), or an explicit skeleton if a human reads it. Then work backwards: what does the model need in context to fill that shape truthfully, and what judgment calls does filling it require? Writing the contract first has a second payoff nobody advertises: it forces you to decide the edge cases (“what goes in severity when the ticket is a feature request?”) — and an edge case you cannot answer, the model will answer differently every time.

Say "cannot determine" rather than guess. </rules> <document> # the material, fenced ... </document> <task> # the ask, near the end ... </task> Output: <exact shape, or schema reference> The fences matter more than their flavour. XML tags, markdown headings, JSON blocks — the model handles all three; what it cannot handle is ambiguity about where the document ends and the instructions resume. XML-style tags have two practical edges: they nest without escaping headaches, and you can refer to them by name (“quote the relevant line from <document>”). Claude models are explicitly trained around them; the other vendors’ models read them fine. Pick a convention, keep it consistent, and never let untrusted input decide where your fences sit — that is the seed of prompt injection, which section four makes concrete.

Every vendor now sells a reasoning dial — extended thinking budgets, effort parameters — that lets the model work through intermediate tokens before answering. It is a real capability gain on genuinely multi-step problems: planning a refactor, debugging from a stack trace, auditing code, maths with dependencies, anything where the answer has to survive several constraints at once. It is pure spend on extraction, classification, reformatting, and summarisation, where the answer is already latent in the input — and it adds latency users feel. The discipline: default it off (or low), turn it up per-route when an eval shows the gain, and cap the budget so a pathological input cannot think its way through your margin. When a cheap model with thinking beats an expensive model without it on your eval, take the cheap one — that result is common now and nobody’s marketing will tell you.

The loop that makes prompts robust is not in the prompt. Production shape: call → validate (schema, invariants, spot checks) → on failure, retry once with the error appended (“your previous output failed validation: amount must be a number — return the corrected object only”) → on second failure, fall back per section eight: different model, degraded mode, or a human. Self-correction with concrete feedback fixes a large share of first-pass failures at the cost of one extra call on the failure path only. Teams burn weeks polishing a prompt toward a 100% first-pass rate that sampling makes unreachable, when validate-and-retry would have shipped the same reliability in an afternoon.

Where does that leave “prompt engineering” as a craft? Smaller and more valuable than the course-sellers claim: it is requirements analysis (the contract), information design (what goes in context, in what order), and test authorship (the eval that catches the regression). The mystique — magic phrases, role-play incantations, “take a deep breath” — measured out to noise or tiny effects on modern models, and every hour spent there was an hour not spent on the loop, which is where reliability actually comes from.

The pivot chapter. Tool use is where text prediction becomes action, where safety stops being philosophy and becomes executor design, and where this book's single most repeated sentence — the model asks; your code decides — earns its repetition.

Tool calling is the mechanism that turns a text predictor into something that can act. The part nobody tells you: the model never executes anything. It asks. Your code decides. Everything serious about tool use lives in that gap.

The protocol, one pass: you send the request with a tools array — each tool a name, a description, and a JSON Schema for its parameters. If the model decides a tool would help, it stops generating prose and returns a tool_use block: the tool’s name plus arguments conforming to your schema (strict mode makes “conforming” enforced rather than probable). Your code looks up the real function, executes it, and sends the result back as a tool_result in a new user turn — replaying the whole transcript, because section two. The model reads the result and either answers, or calls another tool. That cycle, repeated until a stop condition, is the entire runtime of every agent you will ever ship.

The tool-use cycle from section four, as a walkable machine. Step through a realistic run: a user asks about an order, the model plans, your executor acts.

Request goes up. Your code sends the transcript: system prompt, tool definitions, and the user message — “Where is order ORD-88412?” The model reads all of it as one context.

Model elects a tool. Instead of prose, the response is a structured request: call search_orders with these arguments. stop_reason: "tool_use". The model has done nothing except ask.

Executor gatekeeps. Your code — not the model — checks the argument against reality: format valid, tool allowed for this route, caller permitted to see this order, budget remaining (steps, spend, wall-clock).

The real call happens. The executor runs the actual query with its own credentials, scoped to what this agent is ever allowed to touch. The model’s text had no power here; your code did.

Result becomes context. The executor truncates/shapes the result (a 300KB dump would eat the window), wraps it as a tool_result, and appends it to the transcript. Errors would take this same path — as instructive content, not exceptions.

Model reads and decides. The grown transcript goes back up. The model sees the result and chooses: another tool call (loop again), or a final answer. This decision is the whole “agency” — everything else was plumbing.

Terminal turn. The model answers in prose, grounded in the tool result: “ORD-88412 shipped; arriving Aug 11.” stop_reason: "end_turn". The loop exits on this — or on budget exhaustion, or on the agent saying “can’t be done,” and all three exits are yours to enforce.

The chapter with the industry's most oversold acronym. Retrieval is search engineering in service of grounding, it is frequently the wrong first choice in 2026, and both of those claims come with decision tables.

Retrieval-augmented generation is a grand name for a plain move: find the right text, put it in the context window, ask the question. The engineering is in “find the right text” — and in noticing when you should skip the finding and just send the document.

Section one left you with the rule — facts move from weights to context — and an unanswered question: what happens when the facts live in a corpus too large to send? RAG is the answer’s name: at question time, retrieve the relevant slice of the corpus, augment the prompt with it, generate grounded in what was retrieved. The model is the last and least adjustable stage. When a RAG system answers wrongly, the cause is overwhelmingly that retrieval put the wrong text in front of a model that then did its job faithfully on the wrong material. Debug retrieval first, always.

The question the RAG industry prefers you not ask. Context windows run to a million tokens in 2026 — hundreds of pages — and prompt caching (section two) makes re-sending a large stable corpus per-question cheap after the first write. So: if the whole corpus fits comfortably and changes slowly, send the whole corpus. No index to build, no chunking to tune, no retrieval to miss — the model sees everything, including the cross-references a retriever would sever. An employee handbook, a product’s docs, one codebase’s core: cache it and stop. Strip the term to what ships: an agent is a model, a set of tools, and a loop that runs until a goal or a budget. You built the pieces in sections two through four. This section is about the loop’s pathologies, the orchestration patterns that survive contact with production, and the permission design that lets you sleep.

Define it operationally and the fog lifts: workflows are LLM calls composed along paths you wrote (classify → extract → format: three calls, your control flow); agents hand the model the control flow — it reads the goal, picks the next tool call, reads the result, picks again. You pay for that autonomy in variance: an agent finds routes you never wrote, both the brilliant kind and the kind that files a support ticket against your own helpdesk. The 2026 consensus, learned expensively: use the least autonomy that solves the problem. If the path is knowable in advance, write it down as a workflow — cheaper, faster, testable, boring. Agents earn their variance where the path genuinely depends on what each step uncovers: debugging, research, multi-system operations.

The single-agent loop needs four things beyond section four’s executor, and every one of them is a limit: a goal the model can test progress against (“make the failing test pass” beats “improve the code” — agents with unfalsifiable goals do not terminate, they meander); budgets (steps, tokens, wall-clock, spend — enforced by the loop, not requested of the model); a terminal condition beyond success, i.e. the agent can conclude “cannot be done with these tools” and stop — otherwise budget exhaustion is your only exit from impossible tasks; and context management, because every step appends tool results to a transcript that must be replayed (section two), and a long run will hit the window. Compaction — summarising the transcript’s middle while keeping the goal, recent steps, and key findings — is now a first-class vendor feature as well as a thing you can hand-roll. What compaction summarises away, the agent no longer knows: a fact discovered at step 4 and compacted at step 30 is gone at step 31, which is why long-running agents write important findings to an external scratchpad file and re-read it, rather than trusting the transcript to remember.

Resist the org-chart fantasy — a “CEO agent” delegating to “manager agents” is a lot of token spend re-discovering coordination problems humans already find hard. The decompositions that actually pay are mechanical, not managerial. Subagents for context isolation: a researcher agent burns 80k tokens reading forty files and returns a 2k-token brief; the orchestrator’s context stays clean, and three such researchers run in parallel on independent questions. The fan-out costs real money (every subagent re-reads its own context) and pays only when the subtasks are genuinely independent — dependent subtasks serialise and the orchestra becomes a queue with overhead.

The dispatcher is the pattern you will actually run in production, and it is barely “multi-agent” at all: one deterministic front door — a router — receives every inbound request and hands it to exactly one specialist agent, each with its own model tier, working directory, tool set, and standing orders. The worked example this lesson keeps in its pocket is a personal iMessage orchestrator running exactly this shape: one address routes to a repo agent (frontier model, one repository, PR-shaped output), another to a personal-assistant agent (mid-tier model, calendar and mail tools, no code access), a prefix to a cheap meta-agent that reports on the others’ health. The routing is string-matching, not model judgment — boring on purpose, because a deterministic dispatch layer is testable, loggable, and cannot be talked into routing your email to the code agent by a cleverly-worded message. Note what the pattern is really doing: scoping. Each agent’s blast radius is set by its tool inventory at spawn time, per section four — the personal agent cannot force-push because pushing is not among its verbs.

The failure taxonomy to memorise before your first incident: derailment — an early misreading compounds, twenty faithful steps built on step 2’s mistake (mitigate: checkpoints where the agent restates goal-vs-state, and evals asserting milestones, not just endpoints); loops — retrying a failing action with cosmetic variations until budget death (mitigate: executor-side repeat detection, error messages that propose a different move, and honest tool descriptions per section four); goal drift — long transcripts where the original constraint quietly falls out of attention or gets compacted away (mitigate: re-inject goal and constraints near the context’s end each step — the edges again); injection capture — the agent obeys instructions found in the material it was sent to process (mitigate: section four, structurally, because no prompt fixes this).

The chapter that separates teams that improve from teams that oscillate. Datasets as assets, graders as calibrated instruments, the upgrade problem — and the ratchet rule that turns every failure into permanent institutional memory.

Without evals you are doing vibes-driven development against a stochastic dependency — every prompt edit a coin flip you cannot see land. The eval suite is where your team’s taste gets written down, versioned, and enforced. It is also, quietly, the hardest artifact in this whole discipline to keep honest.

Unit tests assert f(x) == y. That assertion is unavailable to you now: outputs are sampled (section one), often long-form, and frequently have many acceptable values. An eval is the replacement discipline — a dataset of real cases, a grading function, and a score you track across every change to prompt, model, retrieval, or tooling. The mental shift that matters: you are no longer testing for correctness, you are measuring a rate. The question stops being “does it work?” and becomes “what fraction of the time does it work, on which kinds of input, and did this change move that fraction?”

Start embarrassingly small: twenty to fifty cases beats zero by more than five hundred beats fifty — and teams stall for months waiting to afford five hundred. Seed from real traffic the moment you have any (section eight’s logs exist partly to feed this), from the failures you find by hand, and from deliberately adversarial cases: the ambiguous ticket, the document missing the field (section three’s escape hatch, now with a test proving it fires), the injection attempt (section four’s canary). Each case: input, expected outcome at the right level of abstraction, and a note on why it is in the set. Then treat the set like the crown jewels it is — versioned, reviewed, and grown by a standing rule the SRE world would recognise: every production failure becomes a case before the fix merges. A regression suite built this way is a ratchet: the same failure, once seen, cannot return unannounced. (Two mechanical rules keep the ratchet honest: cases are append-only — edit a case’s expectation only with review, never to make a red run green — and the graded set stays out of few-shot examples, or you are training on the test.)

Report rates in a form that matches how the system is used. For a single-shot endpoint, pass rate on one sample is the number. For anything a user retries — or an agent loop that gets multiple attempts — measure pass@k (at least one success in k tries) and its stern sibling pass^k (all k succeed): the first describes best-case utility, the second describes reliability, and the gap between them is your variance made visible. An agent tool that works 90% per call sounds fine until the workflow calls it five times and 0.95 ≈ 59% of runs survive. Production reliability compounds against you; evals are where you find that out for less than an incident costs.

The model-upgrade problem — the hardest thing evals do. A new model version lands and half your suite moves: some cases now pass differently, some fail, some pass for reasons your grader was never designed to notice. If your cases asserted surface details — exact wording, list order, phrasing of refusals — you will drown in false regressions and start rubber-stamping diffs, which is how real regressions ship. The countermeasure is a design rule: assert behaviour and outcomes, not prose. “Declines and cites the policy” not “says the following sentence”; “all four sections present” not “formatted exactly thus”; “the patch makes the test pass” not “the patch matches this diff.” Then upgrades become what they should be: run both models over the suite, diff the rates per category, read the actual transcripts where they diverge, and make a decision with numbers in hand — including the decision to hold, which caching economics and your fallback chain (section eight) both need to survive anyway.

Two failure modes of eval culture itself, so you recognise them from inside. Benchmark theatre: the public leaderboard score (MMLU, SWE-bench, arena ratings) tells you about the vendor’s model on the benchmark’s distribution; it is a procurement signal, not a substitute for fifty cases from your traffic — the correlation between “best on the leaderboard” and “best on your task” is real but loose, and the loose part is your margin. Goodhart drift: once a number gates releases, pressure accumulates to optimise the number — rubrics quietly reworded, hard cases quietly parked. The countermeasures are cultural more than technical: the eval suite has an owner, changes to cases and rubrics get the same review as code, and the score’s job is to inform the person who decides, not to replace them. The last chapter is the morning after: cost, latency, reliability, observability, drift. What breaks first, in order, and the boring, powerful discipline — version everything, stage everything, watch the rates — that makes an LLM feature just software again.

Everything before this section was about being right. This one is about being right at 2 a.m., under load, at a unit cost the business survives, with enough telemetry to know which of the previous seven sections is currently on fire.

Ship order, learned the hard way by everyone: observability before traffic. A model call is a distributed-systems dependency with unusually rich failure modes, and the only forensic record is the one you write. Log every call: prompt version, model id, full request/response (redacted per your data policy), token usage split by input/output/cache-read/cache-write, latency to first token and to last, stop_reason, tool transcript for agent runs (section six), and a trace id tying multi-call flows together. This is one table and a context-manager in your client wrapper — the section two abstraction earning its keep — and it is the substrate for literally everything else on this page: cost attribution, eval seeding, drift detection, incident review. LangSmith, Braintrust, and the OTel-based LLM tracing stacks sell this assembled; the pattern matters more than the vendor, and the pattern is traces plus graders plus feedback, on live traffic.

Your bill is traffic × tokens-per-call × price-per-token, and the middle term is the one engineering owns. In practice cost programmes run this sequence: measure per-feature unit cost (cost per resolved ticket, per document, per agent run — from the logs above, tagged by route); cache the stable prefix (section two — in transcript-replaying agents this is routinely the largest single line item, and one accidental cache-buster erases it); right-size the model per route — the frontier model earns its price on the routes where the eval gap justifies it, and classification/extraction/routing run happily two tiers down; shorten what you send (verbose tool results, re-sent documents, few-shot blocks nobody re-audited — token diets are real diffs with measurable payback); batch everything asynchronous at half price. Model tiering plus caching plus batch typically moves unit cost by multiples, which is the difference between a feature and a write-off. Do the arithmetic before the launch meeting: tokens-per-interaction × projected traffic × price, against gross margin. Five minutes of multiplication has killed more AI features than any regulation.

Rate limits (429) and overload responses (529-class) are weather, not incidents: retry with exponential backoff and jitter, respect retry-after, and smooth your own burstiness before asking for a bigger limit. The failures that need design are the sustained ones — vendor outage, model deprecation, a regression in a model update (section seven found it; now what?). The pattern is a fallback chain: same model different region, then a sibling tier, then — for the routes that justify it — a second vendor, which your section-two abstraction made a config change and your provider-agnostic transcript types made survivable. Two honesty notes: a cross-vendor fallback is only real if its evals run too (a fallback you never graded is a different product wearing your UI), and degraded modes beat hard failures — retrieval-only answers, “try again shortly,” a queued job — for every route where something useful exists short of the full feature. Write the decision per route; the incident is a bad time to design.

Drift and hallucination monitoring — evals, pointed at the wild. Your offline suite (section seven) grades the world you knew at ship time. Production adds the rest: sample live traffic — all of it if volume is low, a stratified slice if not — through the same programmatic checks and calibrated graders, async, on the batch tier (cheap, and nobody is waiting). And schedule canary runs: a fixed mini-suite fired hourly against production config, so the quiet refresh pages you before the users do.

What actually breaks first, ranked by field experience: (1) cost — a cache-busting prompt edit or a chatty tool result and the unit economics quietly double; the counter is per-route cost alerts, not quarterly bill reviews. (2) A silent model change — vendor-side refresh, or your own “harmless” prompt tweak, shifting behaviour your wording-pinned evals miss and your canaries catch. (3) The long tail of inputs — the 40-page PDF, the message in Turkish, the pathological ticket that blows the context window mid-agent-run; the counter is budgets and truncation strategies chosen on purpose (sections four and six). (4) Injection, eventually — the canary rate is not decoration; treat its first real hit as the fire drill it is. What does not usually break: the model being “not smart enough.” By ship time you validated that with evals — didn’t you — and the failures that page you are, almost always, systems failures wearing an AI costume.
